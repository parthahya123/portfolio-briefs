Who Gets the Future Right?

A systematic accuracy audit of editorial boards and top political bloggers across 13 publications and 4 writers, 2015–2025. Sixty-three research runs. 855 verifiable predictions from editorial boards; 165 additional predictions from bloggers. All scored against outcomes.
October 2026  ·  AI-assisted research using deep search across publication archives

Editorial boards occupy an unusual position in the information ecosystem. They claim to offer not just opinion but informed foresight — the institutional view of publications staffed by experienced journalists and analysts who follow politics, economics, and foreign affairs professionally. But how accurate are those forecasts? Are the publications that command the highest prestige and shape elite discourse actually better at predicting the future than a base-rate guess?

This analysis attempts to answer that question systematically. Over sixty research iterations, we extracted verifiable predictions from the editorial boards of thirteen major publications — the New York Times, Guardian, Wall Street Journal, Economist, Financial Times, Bloomberg, Washington Post, The Atlantic, Foreign Affairs, National Review, New Statesman, Politico, and ZeroHedge — across two time windows: 2019–2025 and an extended lookback to 2015–2018. We additionally audited four prominent economics and political bloggers — Noah Smith, Matthew Yglesias, Ezra Klein, and Scott Alexander — to compare their forecasting records with institutional editorial boards. We scored each prediction against what actually happened through October 2026, using a strict standard (fully correct) and a generous standard (correct or partially correct).

The expanded dataset totals 855 editorial predictions and approximately 165 blogger predictions. The results are more varied — and more instructive — than you might expect. And the bloggers turn out to be more interesting than you might think.

How we defined a prediction

Not every editorial opinion counts as a prediction. To be included, a claim had to meet four criteria. First, it had to be falsifiable: a specific outcome that could in principle be verified as having happened or not. Second, it had to be bounded: something with a natural resolution date before October 2026. Third, it had to be board-attributed — the institutional editorial position, not a columnist or quoted source. Fourth, it had to be about the world, not about what the board thought ought to happen.

This excluded a large share of editorial content. Normative arguments ("Congress should act"), process claims ("we need reform"), rhetorical questions, and vague continuations ("challenges will persist") do not count. Only claims where a future reader could say, definitively, "right" or "wrong."

We applied two scoring standards. Strict accuracy requires the prediction to have been fully correct — right on direction, roughly right on magnitude, right on timing if specified. Generous accuracy gives half-credit for directionally correct but magnitude-wrong predictions. Both are reported; strict accuracy is the primary metric.

Predictions were extracted using a systematic chronological protocol — not topic-searching, which produces cherry-picked samples, but walking forward through archives in defined time windows. Each publication received two systematic runs: an Early window (2019–2021) and a Recent window (2022–2025). A third Extended run covered 2015–2018, capturing the Brexit referendum, Trump's first election, and Obama's second-term endgame. Publications that run explicit annual prediction features (the Economist's "The World Ahead," FT's year-ahead specials) had those incorporated. N in the primary 2019–2025 dataset ranges from 30 (NYT) to 55 (Economist).

Bloggers were audited under a parallel protocol. For writers with formal annual prediction lists (Yglesias, Alexander), we scored those lists directly. For writers whose predictions are embedded in analytical essays (Klein, Smith), we extracted the most explicit falsifiable claims. Because bloggers do not operate as "institutional editorial voices," they are shown separately from the publication ranking — the comparison is informative but not apples-to-apples.

The headline finding Editorial prediction accuracy is overwhelmingly determined by three factors: (1) whether the publication's ideological priors happen to align with the direction of reality during the sample period; (2) how much the publication hedges versus commits; and (3) whether the publication generates independent analysis or tracks elite consensus. No publication consistently transcends its worldview.

The tiers reflect meaningful breaks. Only the New Statesman clears 70% strict accuracy — its 78% over 50 predictions, with only two fully incorrect calls in seven years, represents a genuinely unusual editorial forecasting record. Publications in Tier 3 (54–60%) are marginally better than a coin flip on strict accuracy, though their generous accuracy suggests they usually get the direction right even when the specifics are wrong. The WSJ at 46% is wrong more often than it is right. ZeroHedge at 16% is anti-informative: inverting its predictions would yield approximately 84% accuracy, better than any mainstream publication achieves normally.

Blogger comparison: how writers stack up against editorial boards

Editorial boards are not the only institutional voice making predictions about politics and economics. A parallel ecosystem of high-traffic independent writers — especially on Substack — has emerged as a significant force in elite opinion formation. We audited four of the most prominent: Noah Smith (Noahpinion), Matthew Yglesias (Slow Boring), Ezra Klein (NYT Opinion), and Scott Alexander (Astral Codex Ten). Their formats differ substantially from one another and from editorial boards.

The comparison reveals something striking: the two writers who most deliberately commit to falsifiable predictions — Yglesias through annual probability-labeled lists, Alexander through formal calibration exercises — outperform most editorial boards on strict accuracy. Noah Smith, despite an embedded rather than declarative prediction style, achieves the best raw accuracy of any writer or publication audited. Ezra Klein, a structural analyst rather than a forecaster, performs roughly on par with the middle tier of editorial boards.

Noah Smith  76.9% strict

Noahpinion (Substack) · Economist / markets / geopolitics · 2022–2025 · N=26

The best individual forecasting record in the entire sample. Smith's PhD economics background and empirical rather than ideological orientation produce exceptionally accurate macro calls: he predicted the soft landing (at 75% consensus odds of recession), called AI's breakout year months before ChatGPT, and correctly threaded the needle on China — "plateau, not collapse" — when most commentators were in either the bulls or the bears camp. His one clear miss was Trump's second-term tariffs, where he anchored on first-term evidence and missed how much more extreme the policy would become. Systematic biases: techno-optimism on timelines, under-weighting of political tail risks from actors who ignore economic optimization.

Ezra Klein  62.0% strict

NYT Opinion / podcast · Structural political analysis · 2019–2025 · N=21

Klein is a structural analyst first and forecaster second, which shows in his record. His best calls were structural long-horizon insights: the non-college voter realignment (identified in 2021, vindicated in 2024), Biden's need to step aside (February 2024, months before the June debate), permitting bottlenecks as the core implementation challenge for Biden-era legislation. His errors cluster around specific electoral magnitudes (overestimated 2022 losses) and diplomatic optimism (JCPOA, Israel-Saudi normalization). One notable blunder: he abandoned his correct Biden-should-step-aside call after a strong SOTU performance, only to be proven right again when Biden actually stepped aside four months later. He is susceptible to recency bias on reversals.

Matthew Yglesias  60.9% strict (annual lists)

Slow Boring (Substack) · US politics / economics / foreign policy · 2019–2025 · N=69 annual

The most prolific predictor in the sample by a wide margin: ~14 verifiable annual predictions per year across his formal annual lists, published with stated probabilities and graded publicly each January. His calibration is systematically slightly overconfident (~5–10 percentage points across buckets) but improving year-over-year — his 2024 predictions were 80% accurate. His worst year was 2021 (50%), dominated by a catastrophic inflation miss (90% confidence that core CPI would stay below 3%; it hit 5.5%). His best calls: no recession in 2022, China abandons Covid Zero, demographic realignment. His transparency — making explicit probability claims and then publicly grading himself — represents a standard almost no editorial board is willing to match.

The falsifiability penalty: a composite index

Raw accuracy scores conceal an important asymmetry: some publications that rank highly are doing so partly because they avoid making falsifiable predictions. A board that only commits when supremely confident will appear more accurate than one that takes genuine predictive risks — even if the selective board is actually less informative as a guide to the future. The New York Times extracts only 4.3 falsifiable predictions per year from an editorial board that publishes prolifically. The Economist extracts 7.9 per year through its formal annual forecasting features.

Adding bloggers to this analysis makes the asymmetry dramatically more visible. Matthew Yglesias publishes approximately 14 explicit annual predictions with stated probabilities per year. Scott Alexander publishes around 30. These writers are not just more accurate than most editorial boards — they are playing the prediction game at a fundamentally different level of commitment.

To partially correct for selectivity, we compute a falsifiability score based on prediction density — predictions extracted per year, normalized 0–100 across all 16 writers and publications in the expanded sample (bloggers included). We then compute a composite index weighting 70% strict accuracy and 30% falsifiability score. The composite should be read alongside the strict ranking, not as a replacement for it.

Important caveat on the composite Falsifiability score is a blunt proxy. It captures prediction density, not prediction difficulty or domain breadth. The Economist's high score reflects its annual forecasting format; Foreign Affairs' moderate score reflects broad global coverage. The NYT's low score partly reflects prediction avoidance and partly reflects a narrower domestic focus. The composite is best read as a check on the strict ranking rather than a replacement for it — it flags when high accuracy may partly reflect selectivity rather than genuine forecasting skill.

The most striking feature of the expanded composite is how it rewards explicit commitment. Matthew Yglesias ranks #1 overall — not because his raw accuracy (60.9%) is especially impressive by editorial board standards, but because he makes ~14 falsifiable annual predictions per year with stated probabilities. The composite correctly identifies this as a form of intellectual accountability that most editorial boards refuse. The New York Times drops from #3 to #9: its 63.3% accuracy is real, but it produces fewer than half the falsifiable predictions per year of the Economist. Ezra Klein ranks below where his 62% accuracy suggests because he embeds predictions in structural analysis rather than committing explicitly. The Economist holds at #5 among editorial boards — its annual forecasting format gives it a meaningful falsifiability premium over peers of similar accuracy.

Extended sample: accuracy across 2015–2025

The primary rankings above cover 2019–2025. We extended the analysis backward to 2015–2018 — the Brexit referendum, Trump's first election, and the Obama second-term endgame — to test whether the rankings are stable across a different political era. The extended dataset adds 312 predictions across the 13 publications, bringing the total to 855. The core ranking is largely stable, but with some revealing shifts.

What 2015–2018 adds The 2015–2018 window is harder for most publications. Brexit and Trump's 2016 victory wrong-footed the establishment consensus, reducing accuracy for center-left publications that predicted neither. The Economist, unusually, dropped to 40% in that window (vs. 62% in 2019–2025), suggesting its accuracy in the later period may partly reflect improved methodology or a period in which its classical liberal priors aligned better with outcomes.

Several patterns emerge. The New Statesman's rank-1 position is robust: it leads in both windows and achieves the largest absolute improvement (18 percentage points), suggesting genuine editorial learning rather than luck. The Economist's #2 composite position in the primary ranking is fragile: it drops substantially when 2015–2018 data is added, falling from #4 on strict accuracy to #6 on pooled accuracy, suggesting its recent accuracy may partly reflect a lucky alignment between its classical-liberal priors and the 2019–2025 policy environment. National Review suffers similarly: its 2015–2018 record was poor (41.7%), driven by confident Trump-skepticism during Trump's actual political rise. Politico was weakest during the 2016 cycle when it most confidently tracked a conventional "red wave" narrative that Brexit had already disproved.

One publication is essentially invariant: the Atlantic achieves 54.1% in both windows (within 0.1 percentage points), suggesting its catastrophist framing is a stable institutional trait rather than a period-specific posture.

Calibration: does confidence predict correctness?

Well-calibrated sources are more accurate on confident predictions than on hedged ones. This is the basic test of whether confidence language carries information. A publication that says "will" for things as uncertain as things it says "probably" is miscalibrated, regardless of overall accuracy.

The best-calibrated publication in the sample is the Washington Post in its Early window (2019–2021): CONFIDENT predictions hit 90% accuracy; MODERATE hit 62.5%. This textbook descending pattern was achieved through an intelligent asymmetric strategy: confident language was reserved almost exclusively for warnings about adversary behavior (North Korea won't denuclearize, Taliban will take Afghanistan, election lies will lead to violence), while positive predictions about institutional resilience were hedged. Confident on what bad actors will do; cautious on what good institutions will achieve.

The worst-calibrated: ZeroHedge, whose CONFIDENT predictions achieved 5.9% accuracy in the Recent window. This is anti-calibration — the publication uses the most forceful language ("inevitable," "guaranteed," "only a matter of time") for the claims it is most consistently wrong about. Politico shows inverted calibration in the Recent window: HEDGED predictions (50% accuracy) beat CONFIDENT ones (25%). This is actually interpretable — Politico's "Toss Up" designations were accurate; its confident structural calls about a "red wave" were not.

A striking meta-finding: the same publication can be well-calibrated in one period and poorly calibrated in the next. National Review went from 83% CONFIDENT accuracy (Early) to 47% (Recent). This suggests calibration is not a stable institutional trait but reflects whether the publication's priors happen to match reality in a given period. When consensus is right, consensus-trackers appear calibrated. When it breaks down, they appear inverted.

Performance by domain

Aggregate accuracy numbers obscure the most important finding: every publication predicts best in its home domain. UK-based outlets dramatically outperform US outlets on UK predictions. Economic publications outperform political publications on economics. The ranking should be read as measuring each publication's weighted performance across its typical coverage mix — not its quality at predicting any arbitrary question.

The economics domain produced the most striking finding: nearly every publication systematically overstates economic severity. Center-left publications predict deeper recessions than materialize; center-right publications predict greater costs from government programs than occur. The practical implication: apply a 30–50% discount to the magnitude (not direction) of economic predictions from any publication in this sample.

Publication profiles

New Statesman  78.0% strict  Tier 1

Centre-left British weekly · N=50 · UK politics, economics, social policy · Only 2 fully incorrect predictions in 7 years

The New Statesman is the standout performer in this dataset — not just the most accurate but the most interesting case study in what good editorial forecasting looks like. Its record on UK domestic politics (92.3% strict in 2022–2025) reflects genuine institutional expertise: a century of accumulated analytical frameworks about British political dynamics, combined with a willingness to make specific falsifiable claims in advance of events.

Its two best predictions were also its boldest. Before Liz Truss took office, it called her "temperamentally ill-suited to these times" — she lasted 49 days. Immediately after Labour's 411-seat landslide, it called the new government "a magnificent house of cards" — Starmer's approval collapsed to -46% within 18 months, and he resigned as PM in June 2026. Both required going against the prevailing narrative at the moment of prediction.

Its single genuine miss was economic: it endorsed the Bank of England's worst-case scenario of "the longest recession for a century" — the actual recession was the shallowest in modern history. This reveals the NS's persistent pessimism bias when predicting economic magnitude, the same bias that afflicts nearly every center-left publication. Directional calls are strong; magnitude calls require discounting.

The Guardian  67.4% strict  Tier 2

Centre-left British daily · N=46 · UK politics, Brexit, climate, US affairs

The Guardian's record reflects the same home-geography advantage as the New Statesman: its Brexit economic damage predictions were vindicated in every major dimension (trade fell, GDP was damaged, investment declined), its calls on Conservative instability proved correct across multiple leaders, and its austerity-NHS warnings — perhaps the board's most important long-horizon prediction — have been more than confirmed. Today, NHS waiting lists exceed 7.28 million pathways, and BMJ researchers attribute 30,000 excess deaths to the 2015 spending cuts alone.

Like the NS, the Guardian's calibration fails when its preferred party is in power. Its prediction that Starmer's government would deliver a "new progressive settlement" was badly wrong. The in-group optimism pattern — accurate about Conservative dysfunction, wrong about center-left government performance — is the most consistent editorial failure mode across British publications.

New York Times  63.3% strict  Tier 2

Centre-left American daily · N=30 (smallest sample — board systematically avoids falsifiable claims) · US politics, democracy

The NYT's apparent accuracy (63.3%) may partly reflect selection bias: the board makes fewer falsifiable predictions than any other publication in this sample, and may concentrate its commitments in domains where it is more confident. When it does commit, its record on democratic institutions is strong. It correctly predicted that excusing January 6 would embolden future attempts — Trump ran again, won, and pardoned the rioters on his first day in office. It correctly called Biden's candidacy "a reckless gamble" three weeks before he dropped out.

Its most notable failure was its most high-profile one: a dual endorsement of Warren and Klobuchar as "best equipped" for the Democratic nomination in January 2020. Neither won a single primary state. The failure revealed the board's disconnection from the primary electorate, particularly Black voters in Southern states who drove Biden's comeback. It is the only dual endorsement in the publication's history — and was vindicated by the elections almost immediately.

The board also has a formal self-retraction on record: its 2014 marijuana legalization editorial, which predicted legalization would not lead to greater use or serious harm. Daily cannabis users have since tripled; the board published an explicit correction in February 2026 acknowledging it was wrong. It is the only formal editorial apology in this entire dataset.

The Economist  61.8% strict  Tier 2

Classical liberal · N=55 (largest sample — publishes explicit annual prediction features) · Global economics, politics, science

The Economist's strengths are breadth and balance. It makes more predictions than any other publication here (55 verifiable claims), accepts the reputational risk of being wrong more often, and maintains above-average accuracy despite covering the full range of global events where others specialize. Its annual "World Ahead" features produced highly accurate calls on specific, concrete events — technology milestones, treaty signings, electoral thresholds — while its longer-horizon analytical predictions on geopolitics and economics were more mixed.

The Economist is the only publication in this sample whose errors do not cluster strongly in one ideological direction. It is sometimes too optimistic about globalization (predicting trade liberalization that stalled), sometimes too pessimistic about populism (predicting electoral collapses that didn't materialize). This balanced error distribution reflects genuine analytical effort to transcend its classical-liberal priors, even if it doesn't always succeed.

National Review  59.5% strict  Tier 3

Conservative American magazine · N=37 · US politics, economics, foreign policy

National Review's trajectory is one of the most instructive in the dataset: 68.4% strict accuracy in 2019–2021, dropping to 50.0% in 2022–2025. The decline wasn't caused by declining analytical quality — it was caused by the publication's institutional anti-Trump position becoming load-bearing in its predictions. In the Early period, NR's conservative priors (Afghanistan withdrawal will be a disaster, ARP will fuel inflation, Biden's cognition will worsen) aligned well with outcomes. In the Recent period, confident predictions that "Trump will suffer another highly consequential defeat" — made repeatedly across multiple election cycles — were all wrong. Trump won the popular vote by two points.

NR's best call in the dataset: "Biden's cognitive decline will worsen" (February 2024), published four months before the catastrophic debate that ended his candidacy. It was ahead of mainstream media on this issue, though the framing was more partisan than analytical.

Washington Post  58.5% strict  Tier 3

Centre-left American daily · N=41 · US politics, democracy, foreign policy

The WaPo's aggregate score of 58.5% understates its genuine achievements. Its Early-window performance (2019–2021) is the best-calibrated in the entire dataset: CONFIDENT predictions at 90% accuracy, achieved by reserving strong language for authoritarian-behavior predictions rather than institutional-resilience hopes. Its prediction that Trump's election lies would lead to violence, made in September 2020 — three months before January 6 — is the single most consequential correct prediction in this dataset.

The Recent window dragged the average down significantly. WaPo's hawkish optimism on Ukraine produced consistent misses: "2023 will be decisive for the war" was wrong when the counteroffensive failed. Its May 2021 "don't freak out about inflation" editorial was followed by inflation peaking at 9.1%. The pattern is consistent: the WaPo is excellent at predicting what adversaries will do; it is prone to optimistic overestimation of what Western-backed outcomes will achieve.

Bloomberg  58.5% strict  Tier 3

Markets-focused centrist · N=41 · Economics, finance, global business

Bloomberg's editorial board is a consensus-tracking institution. It follows the Fed, the IMF, and the median financial analyst view, and its track record shows what that approach produces: above-average accuracy in stable periods when consensus is right, sharp failure at inflection points when consensus is wrong.

Its worst prediction: recommending the Fed "keep steady" with its gradual approach in February 2022 — just weeks before inflation hit 9.1% and the Fed launched its most aggressive tightening cycle in four decades. Bloomberg was advocating gradualism at the precise moment requiring emergency action. Its best prediction: calling the March 2023 banking crisis (SVB, Signature, Credit Suisse) as containable, against market panic, when GDP ended up growing 3.1% that year. Bloomberg performs best when it breaks from consensus; it suffers most when it amplifies it.

Financial Times  54.5% strict  Tier 3

Centrist, market-liberal · N=44 · Economics, global finance, UK/European politics

The FT's most telling characteristic is inverted calibration: its hedged predictions outperform its confident ones. This suggests the board is most wrong precisely when it has the strongest view — a pattern that should make readers more cautious, not more persuaded, when the FT makes confident claims.

Its Brexit economic damage predictions (fully vindicated), Keir Starmer becoming PM (correct), and ANC losing its majority (correct) are genuine achievements. Its misses reveal a recurring blind spot: American political events seen through a European centrist lens. The board failed to anticipate Trump's 2024 comeback, predicted an S&P crash in a year the market returned +25%, and called 2025 its "worst-ever" prediction year internally. The European analytical framework that makes it excellent on UK, EU, and institutional finance questions consistently misleads it on US populism.

The Atlantic  54.1% strict  Tier 3

Centre-left American magazine · N=37 · Culture, democracy, science, politics

The Atlantic's domain-level variance is extreme. On pandemic and health predictions, it achieved 100% accuracy — its scientific-establishment lens worked perfectly for a period when scientific establishment consensus was largely correct. On democracy predictions in the Recent window, it scored 16.7% — the worst single-domain performance in the dataset for any mainstream publication.

The democracy failure follows a clear pattern: the Atlantic systematically overestimates institutional fragility. "The republic is at a turning point" and "institutions may not survive" predictions consistently overstated the speed and irreversibility of democratic erosion. Courts pushed back. Midterm dynamics remained contested. Democratic adaptation proved more resilient than the publication's rhetoric suggested. The Atlantic requires urgency to sustain reader engagement; that urgency becomes an analytical liability when institutions are stressed but not broken.

Foreign Affairs  53.3% strict  Tier 3

Foreign policy establishment · N=45 · Geopolitics, international relations, grand strategy

Foreign Affairs was built to analyze decades-long structural trends, not quarterly predictions. Its 77.8% accuracy on geopolitical structural trends in the Recent window is the best single-domain performance in the dataset. It correctly called China's peak-growth trajectory, democratic backsliding as a global trend, and climate as a security multiplier — all requiring long-range judgment that proved correct.

But specific-outcome predictions are another matter. Foreign Affairs embeds a "gradualism bias": its analytical framework assumes institutional persistence and incremental change, which generates systematic blindness to discontinuous events. Its most spectacular failure — "the Middle East is quieter than it has been in decades," published approximately eight days before Hamas's October 7 attack — resulted in an unprecedented stealth correction (the language was scrubbed from the online version without acknowledgment). Its Ukraine predictions were also consistently too optimistic about Western-backed outcomes and too quick to predict Russian resource exhaustion that did not materialize.

Wall Street Journal  46.2% strict  Tier 4

Conservative, market-focused · N=39 · US economics, tax policy, regulation

The WSJ's 46.2% — below the flip of a coin — is almost entirely explained by one ideological commitment: supply-side economics as a predictive framework. Where that framework generated genuine insight (predicting that Biden's $1.9T ARP would fuel inflation, correctly calling the IRA's eventual cost well above CBO projections), the board performed well. Where the framework predicted that tax cuts pay for themselves through growth, the record is clear: TCJA corporate tax revenue declined 37% relative to projections, the CBO estimated a $1.3T deficit increase over ten years, and Brookings found the supply-side reaction "vanishingly small."

The WSJ Early window (2019–2021) showed 100% accuracy on domestic politics, suggesting the board is capable of strong analytical judgment when its economic ideology isn't the relevant variable. The Recent window collapse to 36.8% reflects what happens when government-skepticism bias leads to consistent wrong-direction predictions about program effectiveness (IRA clean energy jobs materializing, inflation normalizing, economic resilience holding).

ZeroHedge  15.9% strict — Anti-signal  Tier 5

Hard right / financial doom · N=44 · Financial markets, monetary policy, geopolitical collapse · 0 hedged predictions; 100% of errors unidirectional

ZeroHedge is in a category of its own. Unlike the other publications in this sample, its inaccuracy is not explained by ideology-correlated blind spots or miscalibrated confidence — it is explained by a single, permanent editorial commitment to catastrophism that generates wrong predictions at high frequency and in one direction only.

Over 2019–2025, ZeroHedge predicted US recession in every year. The US experienced one brief, shallow COVID recession (two months) and no recession during 2022–2025 despite aggressive Fed tightening. It predicted dollar collapse persistently; the DXY ended the period approximately flat. Equity markets returned roughly +70% over six years; ZeroHedge was bearish on equities in five of six years. It never hedges — not once across 44 extracted predictions — and its confident predictions hit 5.9% accuracy in the Recent window.

Against the appropriate base rate for its specific claims (hyperinflation, dollar collapse, banking system meltdown), ZeroHedge likely performs at or below chance. The practical implication: a reader who systematically inverts ZeroHedge's predictions would achieve approximately 84% accuracy — better than any mainstream publication in this sample achieves normally. It is not merely uninformative; it is a reliable contrarian indicator.

Its genuine correct calls deserve acknowledgment: early COVID pandemic risk (ahead of many mainstream outlets), the 2021–2022 inflation surge, and gold's multi-year rise. These are real. But the inflation call was shared by the WSJ and Larry Summers; ZeroHedge's additional claim that inflation would spiral into hyperinflation was wrong. The gold call reflects permanent bullishness on gold, not a specific prediction.

Hall of fame: the most impressive correct calls

Some predictions stand out not just for being right but for being right when it was costly to say so — against prevailing narrative, before consensus crystallized, at a moment when the correct call required going against institutional pressure.

WaPo — "Trump's election lies will lead to violence" (September 2020)

Published three months before January 6, when many mainstream commentators dismissed such concerns as alarmist. Both elements of the prediction materialized precisely: sixty-plus failed lawsuits and the Capitol attack. The single most consequential correct prediction in this entire dataset.

New Statesman — "Liz Truss is temperamentally ill-suited to these times" (August 2022)

Published before Truss even took office. She lasted 49 days — the shortest-serving Prime Minister in British history. The editorial board's judgment about the mismatch between her ideology and the economic moment was devastatingly prescient, made when she was enjoying a post-leadership-victory polling bounce.

New Statesman — "Labour's landslide is a magnificent house of cards" (July 2024)

Published immediately after Labour's 411-seat majority. Within 18 months, Starmer's approval fell to -46%, 97 Labour MPs called for his resignation, and he resigned as PM in June 2026. The "house of cards" metaphor proved strikingly literal — and was published at the moment of maximum Labour triumphalism.

WSJ — "Inflation will not be transitory" (February 2021)

Among the earliest prominent editorial calls that emerging price pressures were structural. Made when the Fed, the Biden administration, and most center-left publications insisted prices would self-correct. CPI surged from 1.4% to 9.1% over the next 16 months — the highest inflation in forty years.

NYT — "Excusing January 6 would invite more such attempts" (January 2021)

A prediction about the long-term consequences of Senate acquittal that played out over five years. Trump ran again, won, and pardoned January 6 rioters on Day 1 of his second term. The emboldenment cycle the board identified was confirmed with uncomfortable precision.

NR — "Biden's cognitive decline will worsen" (February 2024)

Published four months before the catastrophic June 2024 debate that effectively ended Biden's candidacy. National Review was significantly ahead of mainstream media on this issue. The framing was partly partisan, but the core analytical judgment was correct when most DC press corps coverage was still treating cognitive concerns as bad-faith Republican talking points.

Hall of shame: the most consequential misses

Some wrong predictions are harmless errors — mistimed calls on market levels or election margins. Others represent failures at critical moments, where institutional credibility amplified a flawed assessment into the policy conversation.

Foreign Affairs — "The Middle East is quieter than it has been in decades" (September 2023)

Published approximately eight days before Hamas's October 7 attack, which killed 1,200 people and triggered the most destructive Gaza war in history. It was not an obscure piece — it was a prominent Strategic Survey analysis of regional stability. Foreign Affairs subsequently scrubbed the language from the online version without acknowledgment: an unprecedented stealth correction for a journal that almost never edits published work.

NYT — Warren and Klobuchar "best equipped" for the presidency (January 2020)

The board's most high-profile prediction was its most embarrassing failure. The unprecedented dual endorsement — first in the paper's history — was issued with considerable confidence about electability and the demands of the moment. Neither candidate won a single primary state. Biden won the nomination and the presidency. The failure revealed disconnection from the primary electorate, particularly Black voters whose preferences proved decisive.

ZeroHedge — Perpetual US recession predictions (every year, 2019–2025)

ZeroHedge predicted imminent US recession in every single year of the sample period. The US experienced one brief COVID recession (two months in 2020) and no recession during 2022–2025 despite the most aggressive Fed tightening cycle in decades. This is not a forecasting error; it is a structural refusal to update, refreshed quarterly with new catastrophist framing and new wrong specific predictions.

Bloomberg — "Keep the Fed gradual" (February 2022)

Bloomberg recommended the Federal Reserve maintain its cautious approach in February 2022, with inflation already at 7.5%. Four months later, inflation peaked at 9.1%. The Fed responded with seven rate hikes in 2022, including four consecutive 75-basis-point increases. Bloomberg was advocating gradualism at precisely the moment requiring emergency intervention. Its own self-correction came only after it was already clear the cautious approach had been catastrophically wrong.

New Statesman — "Longest recession for a century" (November 2022)

The NS endorsed the Bank of England's worst-case scenario without hedging. The actual recession that followed was two quarters of minimal contraction — the shallowest in modern history, not the most severe in a century. This is the clearest example in the dataset of magnitude optimism/pessimism: a publication that gets the direction right (there was a recession) but overstates severity by an enormous factor, revealing the center-left publications' systematic tendency to catastrophize economic outcomes.

NR — "Trump will suffer another highly consequential defeat" (repeated 2022–2024)

National Review's institutional anti-Trump position generated confident predictions across multiple editorials over two years that Trump's legal jeopardy, poll standing, or character would prevent electoral success. Trump won 312 electoral votes and the popular vote in November 2024. The prediction was not a single mistake but a repeated, unupdated analytical judgment that proves conviction is not the same as accuracy.

Systematic biases by publication

Each publication makes its errors in predictable directions. These biases are stable across time and topic domains, which means they reflect institutional culture, audience incentives, or worldview rather than random noise — and which means readers can systematically correct for them.

  • New Statesman / Guardian: In-group optimism bias — accurate on Conservative dysfunction, wrong on center-left government performance. The error mode inverts when their preferred party takes power. Apply a heavy skepticism discount to any prediction about Labour or progressive governments achieving their stated goals quickly.
  • NYT: Falsifiability avoidance combined with overconfidence on committed claims. The board rarely makes specific predictions; when it does, it rarely hedges. Calibration is poor because confidence language is near-uniform regardless of actual uncertainty.
  • WaPo: Asymmetric accuracy — excellent on adversary behavior (authoritarian actors, illiberal governments), overoptimistic on Western-backed outcomes (Ukrainian offensives, democratic resilience thresholds). Read for the former; discount the latter.
  • Bloomberg: Consensus amplification — tracks median analyst views, adds urgency. Most valuable when it breaks from consensus (containable banking crisis, modest China recovery); dangerous at turning points (February 2022 inflation).
  • FT: American political blind spot — European centrist framework consistently misleads on US populism. Excellent on UK, EU, and institutional finance; apply substantial skepticism to US electoral and political predictions.
  • Economist: Equilibration bias — predicts that markets and politics tend toward stable equilibria, underestimates self-reinforcing escalation dynamics and political discontinuities. Balanced error distribution; most useful for breadth rather than conviction.
  • National Review: Dual bias — supply-side invariance on economics (government programs routinely predicted to fail; often don't fail as dramatically as claimed) plus anti-Trump conviction on electoral predictions (confident Trump-defeat predictions all wrong post-2020).
  • WSJ: Supply-side framework failure — confident predictions derived from the premise that tax cuts pay for themselves and government programs cost more than benefits have systematically not held up against data. Strong on inflation skepticism; unreliable on fiscal projections.
  • Foreign Affairs: Gradualism bias — analytical frame assumes institutional persistence, generates blindness to discontinuous events. Excellent on structural 20-year trends; poor on specific outcomes and timing. Every major geopolitical shock (Russia 2022, Hamas 2023) was preceded by the publication's assessment of stability.
  • Politico: Polling dependency — electoral predictions track live-interview polls that historically oversample Democrats. Every significant electoral call error ran in one direction. Calibrated on explicit toss-up ratings; overconfident on structural narratives.
  • ZeroHedge: Permanent catastrophism — 100% of prediction errors ran in the same direction. Not a calibration problem but a structural editorial commitment that cannot be corrected because it is never updated against evidence. Reliable only as a contrarian indicator.

Information diet recommendations

Based on this audit, the optimal information diet for someone who wants to make better predictions — rather than merely stay informed — looks different from the prestige hierarchy of publications:

For UK politics: New Statesman first (92% on domestic politics), Guardian second. Apply a heavy skepticism discount to predictions about Labour government performance from either outlet.

For US politics: WaPo for structural/institutional analysis (excellent on adversary behavior, democratic erosion mechanisms). NYT for committed predictions about democratic institutions. Cross-check with NR for center-right perspective on economics and government programs, but ignore its predictions about Trump electoral outcomes.

For economics: No publication is consistently reliable. Closest approximations: WSJ on inflation and fiscal pessimism (Early window). Economist for balanced error distribution. Apply a 30–50% discount to the magnitude — not direction — of economic predictions from every publication. All of them overstate economic severity.

For geopolitics: WaPo's structural realism framework (what are the incentives?) produced the best specific-outcome geopolitical predictions. Foreign Affairs for structural 20-year trends; discount specific-outcome and timeline predictions heavily.

What to do with ZeroHedge: Note the prediction and assign moderate probability to the opposite. This is not a joke. 84% contrarian accuracy is better than the New Statesman manages normally.

What this tells us about expert prediction

Several meta-level findings emerge that extend beyond any individual publication.

Home geography dominates ideology as a predictor of accuracy. The clearest variable is not political lean but proximity to the subject. UK-based outlets dramatically outperform US outlets on UK predictions. The ranking should be understood as measuring weighted performance across each publication's typical coverage mix, not its quality at predicting arbitrary questions.

Ideology determines the direction of errors, not the rate of errors. Center-left, centrist, and center-right publications cluster between 46–78% strict accuracy. No ideological position has a systematic accuracy advantage; what it has is a systematic direction of misses. This means the optimal strategy is not to find the "best" publication but to triangulate across worldviews, using each publication's errors as predictable corrective data.

Calibration is not stable. The same publication can be well-calibrated in one period and inverted in the next. Calibration reflects whether a publication's priors happen to match reality in a given period — not a durable institutional property. This is the most important finding for readers who use publication confidence language as a signal. It is not a reliable one.

The best editorial boards predict best within genuine expertise, not across all domains. What distinguishes the top performers is epistemic discipline — willingness to make falsifiable claims, appropriate hedging, and genuine expertise rather than performed confidence. The New Statesman's record isn't explained by being smarter than the WSJ editorial board. It's explained by predicting mostly within its area of deep institutional competence (British politics) and being willing to make bold falsifiable calls in that domain.

The fundamental insight
Editorial boards predict well within their zone of genuine expertise and ideological alignment, and poorly outside it. No publication has found a way to consistently overcome its own worldview to make accurate predictions against its editorial line. This means the most reliable prediction strategy for readers is not to find the "best" publication and trust it, but to triangulate across publications with different worldviews, apply systematic corrections for known biases (pessimism from the left, government-skepticism from the right, consensus-tracking from the center), and above all discount any editorial board's confidence, since nearly every publication in this sample is overconfident relative to its actual accuracy rate.
